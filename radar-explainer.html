1

What this thing is

In one line
It is a newsroom for competitors, not a newsletter about them.

Amadeus sells the technology that airlines, hotels and travel agencies run on. Right now, AI assistants like ChatGPT and Gemini are starting to plan trips, and they are beginning to book them too. That matters enormously, because booking is the part Amadeus gets paid for.

Answers Leadership · the strategic view

So somebody has to answer a simple question, every week, with evidence: are these assistants getting closer to taking our business, and what do we do about it? That is the whole job. Everything else in this guide is the machinery for answering it without guessing.

Today nobody owns that question. People notice things and mention them in a Teams chat. The message scrolls away. A salesperson hears a competitor's name in a meeting and tells nobody. Three months later the same fact gets discovered again by a different team.

AI Market Radar is the system that fixes it. It collects what is happening, sorts it, decides what deserves attention, turns it into a recommendation, and puts that recommendation in front of the right person in the shape their job needs.

A dashboard nobody feeds is a graveyard. So the habit comes first, and the tool exists to make the habit cheap.
2

The parts, in order

In one line
Information comes in three doors, gets sorted onto five shelves, and leaves as five different views.

The whole system is four moves, in this order. Every chapter after this one is a zoom in on one of them.

The partWhat it does, plainly
1Getting information inThree separate sources of news, kept separate on purpose so you always know how you learned something.
2Sorting and storing itEverything gets filed under one of five topics, and tagged with which door it came through.
3Making sense of itA robot reads and drafts. A human decides what it means and signs their name to it.
4Getting it to peopleFive different jobs, five different screens, plus regular summaries by email.

That ordering is the important bit. Most companies buy a tool first and then wonder why nobody uses it. Here the routine comes first and the software only removes the boring parts of a routine that already works.

3

How we find signals · the three doors

In one line
Three reporters with three different beats. One overhears, one reads everything, one test drives the products.

A signal is just one thing we noticed. A headline, something a customer said, a test result. Every signal enters through exactly one of three doors, and the door is recorded permanently.

Why bother recording the door? Because it lets you say something powerful later: our salespeople knew about this three weeks before the press did. That single sentence is what convinces salespeople to keep contributing.

Door 1 · The field
What our people hear

Salespeople and solutions engineers sit in meetings with real customers. They hear which competitor got mentioned and which objection keeps coming up. Today that intelligence dies in the room.

ExampleAn airline says a competitor demoed in chat booking last week.
Door 2 · Published
What the world announces

News, press releases, product launches, funding rounds. Public and plentiful. Nobody has time to read all of it, which is exactly why a robot does this one.

ExampleSabre opens its infrastructure to developers.
Door 3 · The lab
What we test ourselves

Once a month we sit down and use the competing assistants ourselves. Same tasks every time. We score how well each one does.

ExampleGemini scored 4 out of 5 on planning a multi city trip, up one point since June.
Why three and not one. A press release tells you what a company wants you to think. A sales meeting tells you what customers actually believe. A hands on test tells you what is genuinely true. You need all three, and mixing them into one pile destroys the distinction.

Door 2 is fully automated and running today. Door 1 needs a form inside the tool salespeople already use. Door 3 is deliberately kept human, because judgement about whether a booking flow actually worked is the part a machine cannot fake.

4

The five shelves

In one line
Five topics, chosen because each one is a way we could get hurt.

Every signal gets filed under exactly one of five topics. These are not random categories. Each one is a distinct route by which Amadeus could lose relevance.

1
AI assistant platforms

ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Copilot, Mindtrip. We track what they can actually do from start to finish, not what they announce.

2
Rails and infrastructure

The other companies that move bookings around behind the scenes. Sabre, Travelport, Duffel. These are the direct competitors.

3
Protocols and standards

The plumbing standards that let an AI assistant talk to a booking system. Boring sounding, enormously consequential. Whoever sets the standard sets the terms.

4
Suppliers going direct

Airlines and hotels connecting straight to AI assistants and skipping the middle entirely. This is the route where we simply get bypassed.

5
Payments and checkout

Paying inside the chat. If an assistant can book but somebody else takes the payment, the win is hollow. So payments is a topic in its own right, not a footnote.

Why this beats a single news feed

Answers Product · where is the market heading

A news feed answers the question what happened. Five shelves answer a much better question: did anything move on a front where we are actually exposed? Watch which shelves fill up over six months and you can see the direction the market is travelling, which is the thing a roadmap actually needs. If a shelf goes quiet, that is information too. A feed can never tell you that.

5

How the robot works

In one line
A very fast intern who reads the news every morning, throws away almost all of it, and leaves you a short list.

This is the piece that is genuinely built and running. It is a small program called radar_collector.py. Here is exactly what it does, in order, with no steps hidden.

1
It asks twelve specific questions

Not one broad search for travel news. Twelve narrow searches, one per company or topic we care about. Each search already knows which shelf its results belong on.

Why it matters: results arrive pre sorted, before anything has to classify them.
2
It fetches public news feeds

Ordinary public news feeds over the internet. No passwords, no scraping, nothing private. If one search fails, the program keeps going with the rest.

Why it matters: a partial answer today beats a perfect answer never.
3
It cleans up the text

News feeds add clutter, like repeating the headline as the summary and gluing the publication name onto the end of every title. All of that gets stripped.

Why it matters: the result reads like a briefing instead of a feed dump.
4
It throws away anything older than 45 days

A hard cut off. Older than that is history, and history belongs in a different kind of document.

5
It throws away anything unrelated

A broad news search drags in stock tips and listicles. A simple word check removes the obvious junk. It is deliberately generous, because this is a noise filter, not the real thinking.

6
It removes duplicates

The same headline often arrives through several searches. It compares a simplified version of each title and keeps the first one.

Honest limit: it catches identical headlines, not two different articles about the same event. Step 8 fixes that.
7
It tags each item twice

First the shelf, correcting the default when the words say otherwise. Then something more interesting: it looks for three kinds of event. Funding, meaning somebody raised money or bought somebody. Partnership, meaning two companies joined up. Launch, meaning a real new capability shipped.

Why it matters: those three events are the ones that force us to reconsider how closely we watch a company. See chapter 6.
8
Then Claude reads the whole batch

This step is optional and it is where the quality jumps. The entire list goes to Claude in one go, with clear instructions. Claude throws out what the word matching wrongly kept, fixes shelves that got assigned incorrectly, rewrites every entry as a single sentence explaining why an analyst should care, and re tags the three events, this time only for things that really happened rather than articles discussing them.

Safety net: if Claude is unavailable for any reason at all, the program silently falls back to word matching and tells you which mode it used. It never breaks.
9
It writes a small file, and the dashboard picks it up

The results are saved next to the dashboard. Open the dashboard and the new items are already there, marked with a green pulsing LIVE badge so nobody confuses real collected news with the example data.

Last real run: 14 signals kept, 7 of them flagged as launches, including Mindtrip, Sabre, Navan and Travelport.
The most important thing to understand about the robot. It never decides anything. It gathers, filters, sorts and drafts. Every judgement, and every signature, belongs to a person. The machine buys coverage. It does not buy credibility.
The technical version, for anyone who wants it
Python standard library only, so it runs anywhere with no installation. Twelve Google News RSS queries, a 45 day window, a maximum of 3 items per query and 14 overall. Recency, relevance and duplicate filters run in that order. Category and trigger tagging by keyword lists. The optional triage pass calls claude-opus-5 with a strict JSON schema so the response shape is guaranteed, checks explicitly for a refusal, and catches every exception back to keyword mode. Output is a JavaScript file the dashboard loads if present and ignores if absent.
6

Deciding who is worth watching

In one line
Everyone gets a score out of six. The score sets how often we check on them. A doorbell can override the schedule.

You cannot watch everybody closely. Pretending otherwise is how intelligence teams end up knowing a little about a lot and nothing worth acting on. So attention gets rationed, out loud, using a rule anybody can inspect and argue with.

Step 1 · Three questions, two points each

  • Could this company change something we decide to build in the next three months?
  • Do they touch a part of the business we actually earn money from?
  • How fast are they shipping real things?

Score each from 0 to 2. Add them up. Maximum six.

Step 2 · The score sets the rhythm

ScoreGroupWhat that means in practice
5 or 6Always onConstantly watched. Appears in every summary. Example: OpenAI, Google, Sabre, Mindtrip.
3 or 4WeeklyChecked once a week. Example: Perplexity, Travelport, Duffel.
0 to 2MonthlyA quick sweep once a month. Example: Expedia, regional players.

Step 3 · The doorbell

Schedules are useless if something big happens on a Tuesday. So four events ring a doorbell and force an immediate review no matter which group a company sits in: they raised money or got bought, they partnered with someone, they launched something that genuinely changes what they can do, or one of our own salespeople ran into them in a live deal.

The last one outranks the other three. And this is where the robot connects to the rule: it spots the first three automatically and files them into a queue for review.

Step 4 · Attention is not the same as depth

Being watched closely does not automatically earn the full hands on testing treatment. That is expensive, so it is reserved for companies that could really change what we build. Sabre is watched constantly but tested lightly. Mindtrip is watched constantly and tested thoroughly.

Step 5 · Rescore every quarter

Everyone gets rescored on the same three questions four times a year. The doorbell catches sudden events. The rescore catches the slow drift, which is the thing that quietly makes a watchlist wrong over time.

How a headline becomes a takeaway

In one line
What happened, what it means, what to do about it. Three separate things, never mixed, never blurred.

This is the heart of the whole system. A headline is not intelligence. Intelligence is a headline that has been connected to other things, interpreted by somebody accountable, and turned into a recommendation.

Layer 1
The fact

What happened, with a source and a date. Never edited to fit a story. This is the only layer a machine is allowed to produce alone.

Layer 2
The meaning

What we think it means, and how confident we are. Written by a person. AI can draft it. A person owns it.

Layer 3
The action

What somebody should now do differently. If there is no action, it was news, not intelligence.

Every record in the system keeps these three in separate columns, so anybody reading can instantly tell which one they are looking at. Blurring fact and opinion is the single fastest way an intelligence function loses credibility.

Answers all four teams · what do I do differently

Layer 3 is the one that matters to everyone reading this. The same event produces a different action depending on who you are. A competitor launching in-chat booking means one thing to Product, which is a gap in the roadmap. It means something else to marketing, which is a claim to test before anyone repeats it. To a salesperson it is an objection that will come up next week. To leadership it is a threat that either changes the plan or does not. One record, four actions, written down separately.

The journey, step by step

StageWhat happens
CaptureSomething comes through one of the three doors and gets its shelf and door tags.
ClusterSeveral signals about the same underlying event get grouped into one story.
ConnectCheck the other doors. Did our salespeople mention this first? Did our test scores move at the same time?
AssessAn analyst writes what it means. AI writes the first draft to save time.
SignThe analyst puts their name on it. Until that moment, almost nobody can see it.
DeliverThe same conclusion reaches five different people in five different shapes.
Why the signature matters so much. It is a byline. A machine can write the words, but a named person is accountable for them being right. That is what makes the difference between a summary people skim and a recommendation people act on.

Who we show signals to

In one line
Same information, five different screens, because five different jobs need five different things from it.

A shared inbox fails because it makes every reader do the sorting themselves. So the system reshapes the same underlying records per role. Everybody also gives something back, which is what keeps it alive.

Four teams were named as the people who will use this. Each one asked for something different. Anywhere in this guide that a shaded blue box appears, that is where their specific question gets answered.

The intelligence analyst

Gives: judgement and a signature.  Gets: the full system, including everything unfinished.
PR

Gives: the questions worth investigating.  Gets: capability differences and test results to defend a decision.

We run the same tests on the same assistants every month, so the scores move in a line you can read. Planning is finished, they are all good at it. Booking is moving about one step every three months. Servicing has barely started. That is the shape of the market, and it tells you what to build and what to leave alone.

PM

Writes how we describe ourselves against competitors whose claims keep changing.

Gives: the gaps in our messaging.  Gets: a claim versus reality view, where every competitor claim carries a verdict from our own testing.
Answers Product marketing · buyers, positioning, messaging

Who is buying. The person deciding used to be the distribution manager. Now there is usually someone in the room who owns AI or digital products, and they only want to know whether a bot can book through us without breaking, and who fixes it when it does.

How we compare. The claims page puts what each competitor says next to what we measured ourselves.

What to say. Anything with a verdict beside it can go straight into a deck, because there is a dated test behind it.

PS
Pre sales and solutions

In front of customers, needing an answer to an objection right now, not next week.

Gives: what they hear in live deals.  Gets: a searchable library of objections with tested answers.
Answers Pre sales · objections, fast enough to win

Search the objection you are about to hear, get the answer that has already been tested, and copy it into an email before the meeting finishes. If it is something new, log it, and it comes back to you as a battlecard. Every answer says how many times it has been heard and which test backs it up.

LD
Leadership

Needs the three things that matter this month, on one screen, in under two minutes.

Gives: direction.  Gets: one screen by default, and full access to everything underneath it by choice.
Answers Leadership · shifts, threats, opportunities

One screen. What moved, what is coming at us, and where the opening is. A strip across the top colours the five topics by how hot each one is, and every card ends with what we are already doing about it. If nothing has changed this month, the screen says so rather than filling itself up.

Only two things are locked

Locked door 1 · What our salespeople told us

The details of a signal from a live customer deal are visible to the analyst, to leadership, and to the team that reported it. Nobody else. If those details travelled freely, salespeople would stop reporting them within a quarter, and the most valuable door would close.

Locked door 2 · Anything not yet signed

Draft conclusions are visible only to the analyst and leadership. Nothing unfinished circulates as though it were settled. The moment the analyst signs, it appears for everybody at once.

One deliberate detail. Everybody can see that a locked item exists, including its title and date. They just cannot open it. A hidden wall feels like a system with secrets. A visible wall feels like a system with rules, and it costs far less trust.
9

What is real and what is a mock up

In one line
The robot is real and running. The dashboard is a working prototype with example data in most places.

Being precise about this is the point, not a disclaimer. Anyone can mock up a screenshot. The interesting question is always which parts actually run.

Genuinely working

  • The collector program, end to end. Real headlines, collected from public sources, filtered and tagged.
  • The optional Claude reading pass, including the safety net when it is unavailable.
  • The dashboard itself. Six pages, five role views, both permission walls, night mode, sharing.
  • All five steps of the attention scoring rule, implemented rather than described.
  • The signing flow. Sign a draft and watch it appear for the other roles.

Designed, not built

  • Salespeople reporting through the tool they already use. That needs an administrator, not new invention.
  • Alerts pushed into Teams. Roughly ten lines of code once a channel exists.
  • Saving anything permanently. Signing and logging reset when you reload the page.
  • Automatic detection of when the field knew something first. Curated by hand today.

There is one more honest limitation worth stating. When the word matching runs without Claude, it occasionally files something on the wrong shelf, and it does not merge two articles about the same event. Both happened in a real run. That is not a flaw to hide, it is the argument for the design: the machine tags, the analyst confirms.

Words you will hear

In one line
Every term this project uses, defined once, plainly.
Signal
One thing we noticed. A headline, a customer comment, a test result. The smallest unit in the system.
Stream, or door
How a signal reached us. Field, published, or lab. Always recorded.
Category, or shelf
Which of the five topics a signal belongs to.
Move
Several signals about the same event, assembled into one story with facts, meaning and actions kept separate.
Assessment
The written judgement about what something means. Always has an author.
Signed
An analyst put their name on it. Unsigned things do not circulate.
Trigger, or doorbell
An event important enough to override the normal review schedule.
Tier
How often we check on a company. Always on, weekly, or monthly.
Assistant Lab
Our own monthly hands on testing of competing AI assistants, using the same tasks each time.
Battlecard
A one page cheat sheet for salespeople, covering how to talk about a specific competitor.
GDS
Global Distribution System. The behind the scenes network connecting airlines and hotels to the places people book. Amadeus is one. Sabre and Travelport are the others.
MCP
Model Context Protocol. An emerging standard for how AI assistants plug into outside systems. It matters because whoever sets the standard influences who gets connected to.
Agentic commerce
An AI assistant completing a purchase on your behalf, payment included, without you leaving the conversation.
NDC
New Distribution Capability. An airline industry standard for selling flights with richer content than older systems allowed.
